# Game-Text Corpus: Targeted Reading and Candidate Techniques

**Status:** These cards come from targeted reading. They are not a complete review of the library, a ranking of the works, or independent reader validation. See the [Source Index](source-index.md) for work titles, source IDs, text languages, and card IDs. The detailed Chinese working notes are retained in the [source-language archive](archive/corpus-techniques.zh-CN.md); they are not part of the normal agent reading path.

## Scope and method

A local index dated 2026-09-24 contained 388 text files: 319 visual novels and 69 other games. The sample was selected purposefully across languages, media types, and scene functions. It included passages with speaker labels, continuous script segments, translations, and database- or synopsis-like material. The sample was meant to find operations worth trying, not estimate how often a technique appears in the library or reassess the user's choice of works.

Citations identify a source ID and a visible segment or location. Line numbers refer to one particular version of a text; merging or reformatting may change them. Translations can support bounded analysis of local scene relationships, information order, and actions, but cannot by themselves support claims about the original-language style. Without a recording or executable build, a script cue is not evidence of what the game actually presented. This package does not distribute game scripts.

### Identify the material before drawing conclusions

- **Continuous scene:** A clear scene or source heading and a continuous sequence of speech or narration support analysis of what happens in that passage, not the arc of the entire work.
- **Merged script:** Keep source headings or filenames. Read within those boundaries. Adjacent excerpts in a merged file are not necessarily adjacent in play and do not establish route, chapter, or playthrough order.
- **Extraction, database, or synopsis:** Check whether speakers, conditions, sources, and order survive. A summary is not prose; a database row is not a continuous scene; missing speaker labels cannot establish character voice.

Source checks for this sample found that GAME-GFL memoir excerpts retained speaker labels and segment names; VN-FM script text retained some cue text but not verified in-game effects; the GAME-AA transcript preserved a written chain of reasoning but not evidence imagery or body animation; a GAME-DE file described itself as dialogue-grouped database rows; and several opening GAME-ARK entries were plot summaries rather than dramatic prose. These limits apply only to the particular material checked, not to every file from those works.

## Candidate technique cards

### CT-01 — Different characters process the same information differently

**Sources:** GAME-GFL, VN-KS, and VN-WA2.

**Observed move and possible effect:** Characters respond to the same topic by attending to different parts, reframing it, or moving the conversation in different directions. This distinguishes voice through judgment and response behavior, not only catchphrases or sentence length.

**Try when:** A consequential conversation needs to carry information, relationship, and character attention at once. Note each person's response move—answer, reframe, question, joke, avoid, or act—and check that the next action follows from it.

**Counterexample:** If every line is a clever retort, characters become joke machines. Do not fabricate misunderstanding just to differentiate them. The WA2 evidence is an English transcript and cannot establish the Japanese original's phrasing.

**Evidence:** Cross-work candidate supported by three local passages; optional, not a requirement for every conversation. See evaluations E01, E06, and E07.

### CT-02 — Use tangible facts to keep a gap in understanding readable

**Sources:** VN-E17, VN-MLA, and VN-EA.

**Observed move and possible effect:** After a strange encounter, perception shift, or identity gap, bodily sensation, a clock, an object, or another person's actual response gives the player something to check without supplying the full explanation. VN-EA first establishes an ordinary goal and then lets sensation, time, signs, and communication stop agreeing.

**Try when:** The player should remain uncertain with the protagonist but still be able to follow the next action. Choose one or two facts that narration cannot simply revoke; let each check lead somewhere.

**Counterexample:** Decorative clues do not clarify anything; explaining every mechanism at once destroys the unknown; a pile of unrelated anomalies leaves the player guessing at author rules. VN-EA's sample is one opening scene, not the whole language-learning route.

**Evidence:** Cross-work candidate from three English scripts or translations. It supports local information order and visible checking behavior, not original-language style, in-game timing, or player response. Do not turn verification into a fixed checklist. See E03 and E08.

### CT-03 — Give recurring story material a new use in a changed relationship

**Source:** GAME-GFL, memoir_10_1 through memoir_10_3.

**Observed move and possible effect:** The exaggerated “Sanyaya” assassin story is first a running joke, later part of a storytelling relationship between a child and a doll, and then a name PPK uses to identify a mission target. The repeated material matters because speaker, listener, use, and current risk change.

**Try when:** A familiar story, gesture, or phrase can return with a changed speaker, audience, purpose, or cost. Let a character's choice show the change; the first appearance need not be a solemn foreshadowing.

**Counterexample:** If the recurrence only adds another repetition or the narrator explains the meaning, it has not changed function. This is evidence from one work, not a general rule.

### CT-04 — Let environmental description create a new relationship state

**Source:** VN-FM, scenario2.ks, “Weeping Manor” segment.

**Observed move and possible effect:** The manor's courtyard, dust, webs, and disrepair establish time passing and a servant's loneliness; a new arrival disrupts her expectation of being alone. Smell, food, and blood later affect how she sees the stranger and whether she approaches.

**Try when:** A place, as seen by a viewpoint character, can change what they expect or do. Connect one environmental detail to the character's situation, then let a person, object, or sound change its meaning.

**Counterexample:** A long description that changes no expectation, action, or interpretation delays the scene. An explicit emotional conclusion can also leave the details with nothing to do. English script cues do not establish actual sound, timing, or camera work.

**Evidence:** Single-work case; useful to compare with E03 and E13. It does not mean every scene should open with extended environment description.

### CT-05 — Make a bodily clue a question to test, not a verdict

**Source:** GAME-AA, Apollo Justice 4-1.

**Observed move and possible effect:** Apollo notices a witness touching their neck while discussing a particular part of the testimony. He connects the movement to that claim, asks about the witness's state afterward, and tests the hypothesis against evidence. Observation, hypothesis, and question appear in sequence.

**Try when:** A character's action can give the player an investigative entry point. Tie it to a specific statement, allow a question or test, and leave room for misreading until other evidence narrows the possibilities.

**Counterexample:** “Looks nervous” does not prove lying. If the inference depends on animation or expression missing from the transcript, text alone cannot show that the clue was fair.

**Evidence:** Single-scene interaction case; check E04 and E08. In-game evidence and input require the game itself.

### CT-06 — Let characters compare explanations at a chapter end

**Source:** VN-HIG, Ep1/omake_01.txt.

**Observed move and possible effect:** After the chapter's attacks and calls have appeared, characters offer human-action, curse, or mixed explanations, ask about motives and beneficiaries, fail to agree, and turn toward the next chapter. Their disagreement reopens the evidence and makes both grounds and gaps visible.

**Try when:** The chapter is over and the player has already encountered the relevant evidence. Have each explanation point to an observation, retain what is not yet known, and close on an investigation, promise, or next-chapter question.

**Counterexample:** If the player still needs to make the inference, characters may answer it too early. If the work promises immersion, direct talk about the player or the work may break its contract. Repeating theories without their evidence creates noise, not a clue map.

**Evidence:** Single-work structure case. The source file repeats the scene later in an updated index, so it is one case, not independent support.

### CT-07 — Use one remembered line to connect a new chapter to the same thread

**Sources:** VN-9N3 and VN-9N4.

**Observed move and possible effect:** The next episode names the immediately preceding farewell, then asks about the Overlord and continues from the protagonist's earlier intervention. A specific callback can avoid restating world setup while adding new pressure.

**Try when:** Order and state are stable. Pick one detail the player has actually seen and that still has consequences; react to it before adding the next problem.

**Counterexample:** If order can change, earlier material may be unread, or state resets, an assumed memory becomes a continuity error. Standalone episodes may need a short state reminder instead of a callback.

**Evidence:** Single-work chapter-transition case. It does not establish actual play order, branch-save rules, or player response, and does not create a rule that every chapter must revisit the last one.

### CT-08 — Let a shared object carry different immediate uses

**Source:** VN-SWAN, ssb008.ks segment.

**Observed move and possible effect:** A bag of sunflower seeds moves among characters as food, a planting plan, an object to question, and a prompt for memories of the place. The handoffs connect practical survival with different ideas about the future without requiring everyone to agree.

**Try when:** A small object has real use in a group scene. Let a few people handle it differently, and allow action to show their wishes.

**Counterexample:** If every handoff exists only to announce a theme, or everyone states the same symbolism, the staging feels forced. Ordinary scenes do not need hidden foreshadowing.

**Evidence:** Single-work group-scene case; related to CT-01 but focused on physical handoffs, not response to the same information.

### CT-09 — Let options show what the player wants to ask about now

**Source:** VN-AMN, scenario/525.iet.

**Observed move and possible effect:** Faced with a dangerous overnight invitation, the protagonist can ask whether staying home is safe, what the death prediction means, or express concern about Ukyo. These options distinguish attention—safety, explanation, or relationship—before choosing a major action.

**Try when:** Several questions or observations are reasonable responses to the same situation. Give each a relevant reply and converge only where shared information allows it.

**Counterexample:** If options are paraphrases or all lead to a generic reassurance, they add little. Do not disguise a persistent state change as a harmless question. This extraction has misaligned labels, a repeated block heading, and truncated dialogue, so it cannot establish full branch mapping or later consequences.

**Evidence:** Single-work option case. It illustrates one question-focused option type, not a requirement that all choices express emotion.

### CT-10 — State a choice's reason so others can respond to the trade-off

**Source:** GAME-MEA, Mass Effect: Andromeda, mer_epi_0110_nexus_leaders_d.txt.

**Observed move and possible effect:** The player chooses an interim ambassador and can argue for Pathfinder stability, representation by outpost builders, Krogan seats, or including Angaran leadership first. Other characters respond from empowerment, military stability, representation, existing problems, and power distribution. The choice makes the standard behind a decision visible.

**Try when:** A public decision has competing interests. Pair an action or candidate with a concise reason; let another character address that reason with support, cost, or objection.

**Counterexample:** If the reason is cosmetic while a hidden score alone determines the outcome, the player is misled. If every character gives an equally long “balanced” speech, the scene becomes a debate written by the author.

**Evidence:** Single-work RPG decision case. Database order is not actual play order and does not prove voice performance or downstream world state. Distinct from CT-09: this card concerns policy trade-offs, not what to ask.

### CT-11 — Let an absurd rule keep a real argument moving

**Source:** VN-LB, merged passage.

**Observed move and possible effect:** To stop a fight, Kyousuke imposes a rule about using useless objects as weapons. Masato takes it literally and reaches for a cat; Rin takes the cat away, changes the rule, and redirects the dispute toward other objects. The original grievance remains beneath the escalation.

**Try when:** The scene already permits farce and the characters willingly engage with a game-like constraint. Make the rule visible and executable; let each use of an object change who can act or what the next beat is.

**Counterexample:** An animal or real injury may alarm rather than amuse. Serious trauma or unequal harm can make a sudden prop fight feel flippant. An object that changes nothing makes escalation random.

**Evidence:** Single-work comedy case based on dialogue transcription; physical action and timing are not available.

### CT-12 — Let a practical errand and care within a relationship coexist

**Source:** GAME-TOD, Tales of Destiny.

**Observed move and possible effect:** Garr brings Stahn to find Chelsea because Alba needs a button sewn back on his trousers and worries that she gets lost. Chelsea objects to being treated like a child, then agrees to help one last time. The task remains real while concern, independence, and teasing coexist.

**Try when:** A practical task gives an established relationship something to do. Let people disagree about the task, timing, or responsibility, and answer the concern with an actual decision.

**Counterexample:** With a power imbalance, repeated requests can read as pressure rather than intimacy. Laughter should not erase one person's protest. A task invented only to extract emotion feels like a device.

**Evidence:** Single-work daily-relationship case. Unlike CT-08, it focuses on responsibility negotiated through a recurring task; not every warm scene needs a hidden worry.

### CT-13 — Distinguish the player, protagonist, and name variable

**Sources:** GAME-ONE, OneShot; VN-DDLC, chapter-one script.

**Observed move and possible effect:** In OneShot, Niko asks about “Player,” is told Player is a god in the world, remembers the name from a computer, and tries to communicate; the player's answer changes immediate dialogue. In the cited DDLC passage, [player] functions as a protagonist-name variable in ordinary club conversation. A name token alone does not prove that characters perceive the external player.

**Try when:** First define who the player, protagonist, name variable, and system refer to. If a character addresses the external player, establish a readable channel and let input change a bounded response. If a token merely renames the protagonist, write the scene as an ordinary character relationship.

**Counterexample:** Without a channel, claims about unentered thoughts feel like mind-reading. If input never affects the response, direct address may be decoration. Treating every name variable as fourth-wall awareness damages immersion.

**Evidence:** Contrast case, not a universal technique. OneShot's transcript cannot show UI or input implementation; the DDLC excerpt does not establish its entire metanarrative design.

### CT-14 — Let new evidence change the next move in an argument

**Source:** VN-SGATE, Steins;Gate.

**Observed move and possible effect:** After Mayuri recognizes a metal Upa in the news, Rintaro connects it to the present event and asks Suzuha whether she already knew. She apologizes, admits what she withheld, and points to an earlier video message rather than explaining everything at once. The evidence and the next speaker successively redirect the exchange.

**Try when:** A record, object, or inspectable fact can ground a question after concealment is discovered. Let the person challenged respond locally before deciding whether to reveal more context.

**Counterexample:** An authorial “document” that changes no character's judgment is just an exposition prop. A successful plan does not automatically justify the suffering it caused.

**Evidence:** Single-work high-conflict case. The transcript cannot establish pauses or player response; not every argument needs a secret file or twist.

### CT-15 — Treat continued questioning after a refusal as conflict behavior

**Source:** VN-ASE, A Summer's End: Hong Kong 1986.

**Observed move and possible effect:** Michelle says she must go home, repeatedly says she cannot stay, and asks Sam to stop asking. Sam continues to invite her to stay and talk; she eventually leaves and faces her mother's questions. The text keeps the refusal intact; the continued request creates pressure rather than silently turning into consent.

**Try when:** The work intends to show mismatched boundaries, not listening, or a character unable to speak freely. If pressure is part of the conflict, let it have a cost and do not have narration declare that the other person secretly wanted it.

**Counterexample:** If the goal is safe intimacy, persistence conflicts with an explicit refusal. A later “playing hard to get” explanation would erase earlier evidence. For consensual closeness, do not copy this repeated request pattern.

**Evidence:** Single-work risk case from an English translation; script alone cannot establish how voice or animation affected the scene. Use as a boundary check, not a positive romance template.

### CT-16 — Let a long recollection follow a present question

**Source:** VN-EDG, The Eden of Grisaia.

**Observed move and possible effect:** Alone after the students leave, Yumiko asks what she should do next, recalls two things Yuuji said, considers their meaning, repeats one line, and leaves the classroom. A clumsy eavesdropping joke just before her solitude lets the serious passage land differently. The English text also explains its own meaning directly, so it is not a model of restraint.

**Try when:** A character has an unresolved present problem and relevant past experience. Choose one or two concrete memories, show why they return now, let them alter the character's view, and end with a present action—or an attention or stance change if no decision is needed.

**Counterexample:** Memories that all argue the same theme make the monologue an essay; mentor-like aphorisms make the character a mouthpiece; explaining the conclusion early and repeating it later slows the scene. The preceding comedy depends on this particular relationship and should not be inserted mechanically into grief.

**Evidence:** Single-work case from one continuous English script passage. It cannot establish page layout, voice timing, or the whole character arc.

### CT-17 — Let a route through space reveal a character's situation

**Source:** VN-NAR, Narcissu.

**Observed move and possible effect:** The narrator follows floor markings past an elevator, nurses' station, and sitting room, noticing flowers, restricted windows, and wristbands. A blue wristband from the past and a white one now alter the familiar place; a conversation about floor rules makes her realize she already knew some of them. Movement, objects, and response gradually make the visit personal.

**Try when:** Place, route, or return changes what the character understands. Select a few details in the order they are encountered; connect one to past impression or current identity, then let dialogue or thought revise its meaning.

**Counterexample:** Listing furniture creates a tour, not a scene; making every object a secret clue feels over-plotted. This hospital scene's heavy shared rules are not a content template for a school farewell.

**Evidence:** Single-work Japanese-text case; it supports local ordering, not judgments about English style, game pagination, or whole-work pacing. Compare with CT-31.

### CT-18 — Make a misunderstanding readable through a local attempt and response

**Source:** VN-EA, The Expression Amrilato, am001a.rpy.

**Observed move and possible effect:** Rin sorts possible explanations, tests her taste and the possibility of a camera, then asks a girl about the camera and repeats a similar-sounding word. A gesture suggests she used the question form incorrectly; she tries another phrase. One response narrows the local misunderstanding without making conversation fluent.

**Try when:** Curiosity, teaching, first contact, or light comedy lets a character try and revise. Give one misunderstanding a concrete attempt and a visible response; distinguish observation from tentative inference and allow the exchange to remain incomplete.

**Counterexample:** If a response does not distinguish between guesses, a “correct” inference may feel unfair. Repeatedly listing theories turns daily interaction into a puzzle. In psychological horror, this method can explain away the unknown; in high-stakes medical or trauma scenes, do not present an unverified guess as diagnosis.

**Evidence:** Single-scene case from an English extraction with translation provenance not established; it cannot establish voice, pauses, or the full teaching design. E03 is a deliberate counterexample exercise, not a transfer recommendation.

### CT-19 — Let the next scene carry a prior emotional choice into a new response

**Sources:** VN-KKC, KIRA KIRA Curtain Call; VN-ALP, A Little Lily Princess; GAME-CT, Chrono Trigger.

**Observed move and possible effect:** A confrontation about a performance continues when the protagonist asks Kazu whether she was wrong; he affirms her impulse while naming the cost of smashing a guitar. In A Little Lily Princess, Sara's choice not to give up hope for her birthday meets the actual gathering and a request to let Becky stay. A Chrono Trigger passage also has an earlier loss re-enter a later decision through a clear time reference. A following scene can carry a stance forward, then let another person or new fact change what it means.

**Try when:** Consecutive scenes have a stable reading order and an emotion, question, or promise is still active. Pick it up through a concrete object, question, time result, or response, then add new pressure. Supply only context needed now.

**Counterexample:** If the earlier stance changes no later action, repeated callbacks feel mechanical. If every scene ends with an image and the next repeats it, the structure becomes formulaic. Variable order, unread episodes, reset state, or route differences require conditional text or renewed acquisition of facts.

**Evidence:** Cross-work, cross-media candidate after one original E02 scene exercise and E04 branch-boundary check; retained as an optional scene operation, not a requirement that every adjacent scene echo. The Chrono Trigger file order does not prove in-game route order. Source materials are English extracts/translations and Japanese text.

### CT-20 — Let a competition carry individual goals and teammate interaction

**Source:** GAME-FF9, Final Fantasy IX, hunting festival.

**Observed move and possible effect:** A timed competition establishes scoring and prizes; Zidane wants gil, Freya an accessory, and Vivi a card. Encouragement, assistance, a demand to land the final hit, and post-event praise give the contest room for relationships as well as goals.

**Try when:** A contest, practice, or optional challenge already supports character interaction. State its objective and scoring clearly; let participants show what they want, then make support, competition, and result visible through action.

**Counterexample:** Long dialogue can interrupt tense real-time play. An unwanted entry or reward cannot be excused by team victory. One extracted outcome is not every player's outcome; do not force a reconciliation after the contest.

**Evidence:** Single-work case from Japanese merged script; no proof of precise triggers, player control, timing, alternate results, or player response. An E15 original magic-academy scene was self-reviewed only; it is not an independent test.

### CT-21 — Let repeat inspection respond to the player without making clicks a hidden clue

**Source:** VN-AG, Akatsuki no Goei, 01_aya_004.ks.

**Observed move and possible effect:** Repeated checks of a mirror and toilet use visit counters and produce small character behaviors, such as grooming or folding tissue. The search finds nothing; the protagonist leaves and doubts himself. Later information makes an earlier suspicion worth reconsidering. Repeatable nodes can acknowledge attention without every response becoming evidence.

**Try when:** Exploration permits re-entry and character voice can support small variations. Keep required clues available on every necessary path. Add state only if later visits change an action or response; if later evidence reinterprets an earlier observation, show what was noticed and why it was dismissed.

**Counterexample:** Extra jokes can weaken a scene where time is passing or action is tense. Do not hide required information behind repeated clicks, or force a retrospective clue if later evidence does not actually explain it.

**Evidence:** Single-work case from an English text extraction with truncated lines. Counter thresholds establish that the source text retains counters, not the actual menu, reset behavior, or player experience.

### CT-22 — Let short branches receive a response before shared information resumes

**Sources:** GAME-MEA, Mass Effect: Andromeda; VN-AOKA, Aokana.

**Observed move and possible effect:** In MEA, distinct reasons for killing a leader or protecting a teammate receive separate replies before returning to a common “What do you need?” entry. In Aokana, two reasons for teaching Asuka to fly receive separate responses before the scene returns to shared discussion of flying with Grav Shoes. Each path is answered without making the common text depend on its exclusive reason.

**Try when:** A choice changes only local reason, expression, or attitude and does not change state later scenes must read. Respond briefly to each option, then return to a topic or action established on every entry.

**Counterexample:** If a choice changes knowledge, fact, relationship, promise, object, or risk, keep that difference through conditions, distinct actions, another choice, or in-scene acquisition. Do not flatten persistent consequences at convergence.

**Evidence:** Two-work local comparison, added as an optional structure to the interaction reference after an original branch scene and E04 boundary review. The extractions do not verify in-engine order or persistence; not every choice needs a separate reply.

### CT-23 — Acknowledge the prior event on revisit, then reassess what is still possible

**Sources:** GAME-KOTOR, Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic; GAME-MEA; GAME-ONE, OneShot.

**Observed move and possible effect:** Conditional dialogue can acknowledge that a character was saved or lost, then return to a similar service or task. OneShot supplies a boundary: once the missing sister has been found, searching is no longer the same active task; the conversation shifts to reunion, danger, and an invitation. A callback works best when the current action reflects the changed state.

**Try when:** An NPC remains accessible after an event. Check the outcome, presence, knowledge, relationship, and whether the old task is still active. Use a short conditional acknowledgement if needed; continue the shared service only when it still applies, otherwise choose a new action.

**Counterexample:** Reusing a completed search or menu makes the world feel unchanged. A universal “thank you” cannot settle grief, blame, or a damaged relationship. Rescue does not create a debt of loyalty, and self-blame must not deny the player's action.

**Evidence:** Cross-work local comparison added as an optional interaction operation after an E04 state review. Database order, incomplete branch conditions, and numeric flags do not establish actual play sequence or player response. Not every revisit needs a callback.

### CT-24 — In a temporary handoff, show authorization, scope, and personal dependencies

**Sources:** GAME-GFL-EN, VN-KS, VN-SAKURA-GYM, GAME-MEA, and VN-SHK.

**Observed move and possible effect:** Local handoffs use different grounds: established role, team approval, an owner's direct request, or finding a successor before departure. A contrasting checkpoint scene shows staff refusing a request because they would be accountable, then changing course only after a concrete challenge to authority and responsibility. The story can keep a task moving without making all responsibilities interchangeable.

**Try when:** A character is absent but work or service may continue. Establish the shared duty, qualification, or explicit permission; let the substitute finish the authorized part. Delay only the step that truly depends on the absent person's relationship, access, or private knowledge, and show why. If a justified refusal must be overcome, add a concrete transfer of authority, responsibility, or power.

**Counterexample:** Making every visitor wait for an absent character despite a trusted, authorized substitute artificially blocks the scene. Letting an unqualified person take over everything merely to seem reliable makes authority meaningless. Do not use friendliness to erase accountability.

**Evidence:** Cross-work scene check added as an optional operation after an E01 transfer exercise; the checkpoint is a boundary case, not a handoff example. Extractions do not prove full permissions or player experience. No new mandatory rule was added.

### CT-25 — Let performance outcomes pass through bodies and viewing positions

**Sources:** VN-KKC, KIRA KIRA Curtain Call; VN-SS, Shining Song Starnova.

**Observed move and possible effect:** One performance's failure appears through audience members chatting or checking phones and the singer's hoarse voice and cold sweat. Another looks successful to the audience while Aki hides chest pain; her mother's control-room view adds a separate interpretation. Bodies, observable audience response, and a specific observer can make success, failure, cost, and misunderstanding coexist.

**Try when:** Audience feedback changes a performer's feelings, choices, or the scene's relationships. Use only one or two signals the viewpoint can hear or see; add an observer only when their interpretation affects events. Keep applause, comments, and a narrator's view tied to their sources rather than treating them as objective scores.

**Counterexample:** A quiet or private performance needs no audience. Do not force injury, a quiet pause, or introspection to look realistic. If the viewpoint cannot observe the audience, do not borrow its thoughts. Do not reduce every performance to applause as a score.

**Evidence:** Two-work local comparison and E15 cross-type transfer resulted in an optional presentation check in direction.md. Both are English script extractions; repeated merged material is not independent evidence. No proof of actual performance, camera, audience, or player response.

### CT-26 — Let a response to unfinished work reveal the creator's wishes

**Sources:** GAME-ARK, Arknights; VN-WAGO, The Way We ALL GO. VN-SR, Symphonic Rain, is a boundary comparison.

**Observed move and possible effect:** A fitting of an unfinished dress lets the creator explain the life and aesthetic she hopes for. In another case, a viewer praises a painting but cannot understand the latest unfinished image; that gap draws out the creator's wish to depict something “true” and her doubt that the old painting still matters. Feedback can be practical or interpretive; the maker may explain, revise, or set the work aside.

**Try when:** The unfinished work matters to a character's goal and another person has a natural reason to see, use, or discuss it. Respond to a recognizable detail, then let the creator state a standard or desire in the moment.

**Counterexample:** Forcing a private work into view, or asking for feedback that changes nothing, makes the scene instrumental. Feedback need not lead to obedience or inspirational completion. Symphonic Rain's teacher comments on a performance but does not use a response to an unfinished work to elicit its creator's goals.

**Evidence:** Two local cases, one boundary comparison, and one author self-review transfer; retained as corpus cases only. The Arknights file-to-scene mapping is tentative; the painting is not visible; neither case proves reader response.

### CT-27 — Use a recoverable mistake to make a practice promise real

**Source:** VN-WW, Wanting Wings, chocolate-making lesson.

**Observed move and possible effect:** Fumi calls the class practice, admits her own past failures, and demonstrates stirring. Hina overdoes it and splashes chocolate; Fumi steadies the bowl, checks that little was lost and materials remain, then reassures Hina without denying the mistake. The scene shifts into established teasing rather than showing a second attempt.

**Try when:** A scene involves teaching, practice, or a beginner relationship. State the actual cost and remaining resources first; let a mistake happen, show the action that limits loss, and say what can or cannot be recovered. Continue practicing only if the scene needs it.

**Counterexample:** Do not call a mistake harmless if it injures someone, uses the only material, misses a deadline, or cannot be repaired by the person with power. Do not force a discouraged person to accept comfort or make every beginner error cute.

**Evidence:** Single-scene case; the flirtation depends on an established romance and is not evidence that the lesson caused intimacy. English translation provenance and in-game timing are unverified. No general rule added.

### CT-28 — Check a demonstration through independent use under new conditions

**Source:** VN-KARA, KARAKARA.

**Observed move and possible effect:** After a failed cooking attempt and a broken cheap vase, Aisia watches Lucia demonstrate cleaning and sees Leon cook. The following morning Aisia independently makes clam chowder from leftover ingredients; others taste it and give specific feedback. The outcome makes learning visible and marks a resource boundary, though one demonstration-to-success may be a comic character trait rather than realistic instruction.

**Try when:** The learner has seen a demonstration and later has a chance to apply a similar skill independently. Change one condition slightly and show the result through a product, use, or specific feedback. Add practice steps for realistic growth; use leftover or backup materials only if they are genuinely available.

**Counterexample:** Complex skills that jump from one demonstration to reliable performance may lose credibility. A single finished product cannot prove a skill involving long-term judgment. Do not convert every encouraging moment into a practical exam.

**Evidence:** Single-work case, compared with CT-27's immediate in-class recovery. English extraction provenance and actual learning time are unverified; no general rule added.

### CT-29 — Give feedback by subskill and retain what is not yet stable

**Source:** VN-AOKA, Aokana, Episode 1 flight training.

**Observed move and possible effect:** Asuka learns posture, lift, and balance in stages. She can get airborne but cannot yet balance; later feedback names the actions she can control while noting that stability and competition technique remain weak. Progress becomes observable without turning the skill into a binary “learned / failed.”

**Try when:** Training is part of the scene and a skill has observable components. Choose one current action, show an attempt, and give feedback that addresses it. Advance only one or a few dimensions while keeping other limitations visible.

**Counterexample:** If learning is only background, detailed coaching can read like a manual. If the scene needs an exaggerated action climax, repeated technical breakdown may drain its energy. Safety limits should reflect the actual activity, not become automatic ceremony.

**Evidence:** Single-work case; KARAKARA's rapid next-day success is an adjacent contrast, not a second support case. The English extraction cannot establish actual game order or player response. No general rule added.

### CT-30 — Let repeated rehearsal become a character reaction in the scene

**Source:** GAME-FF9, Final Fantasy IX opening rehearsal.

**Observed move and possible effect:** The troupe repeats a line in the ship play; an estimate of roughly the sixty-fourth attempt and Ruby's complaint make repetition part of the characters' interaction. Repetition can signal comedy, impatience, fatigue, or practice when another character responds to it.

**Try when:** Rehearsing, calibrating, or retrying is already an event the characters experience. Let a character react at a meaningful point—a persistent mistake, a teammate packing up, or a changed goal. Use a count or action; not every failed attempt needs a line.

**Counterexample:** Do not make characters aware of player retries unless the work establishes that contract. A serious immersive scene may not want a fourth-wall joke; a single attempt needs no repetition gag.

**Evidence:** Single-work Japanese-script case; annotations do not prove a player dynamically caused the count. E07 transfer was self-reviewed only; no general rule added.

### CT-31 — Let passing on a rule complete a handoff between characters

**Source:** VN-NAR, Narcissu.

**Observed move and possible effect:** A resident teaches the new narrator a rule passed among people on the seventh floor and asks her to tell future newcomers. Near the ending, the protagonist asks a new nurse to carry a different message to those who remain; the narration explicitly treats it as a rule being passed onward. The structural echo changes a receiver into a transmitter and can create closure through action rather than a thematic summary.

**Try when:** A continuing community actually passes information among its members. Establish who tells newcomers, then let a former recipient pass on a changed or newly relevant piece of information. Show the role shift through a request, hesitation, or action.

**Counterexample:** A one-time instruction is clearer when stated directly. A pessimistic belief, dangerous advice, or rumor needs its source and uncertainty; repetition does not make it true. Distinct from CT-24, which concerns authorized work and accountability rather than peer transmission.

**Evidence:** Single-work Japanese-text structure case and one author self-review transfer. The opening rule contains a heavy claim about food and family burden; treat it as a belief circulated by characters, not as general advice. No general rule added.

### CT-32 — Let a brief acceptance of mistaken identity land in one concrete response

**Source:** GAME-XJ3, Chinese Paladin 3, Penglai side story.

**Observed move and possible effect:** An injured disciple mistakes Xuejian for Chunhua and asks about marriage and an old form of address. Jing Tian has already corrected him, but Xuejian says “I am Chunhua” and gives the requested address; Zixuan then says he can no longer hear. The scene makes the misidentification visible and lets Xuejian choose a brief answer without adding a backstory or explaining her feelings.

**Try when:** The scene clearly shows the mistake to the reader and another character, and correction can no longer change the immediate exchange. Let the responder choose whether to correct or briefly accept the premise, answer one concrete question, then end on an observable consequence.

**Counterexample:** If the person can still understand and decide, or identity affects consent, promises, safety, or later action, going along may take away informed choice. Do not infer that illness makes truth worthless or that the injured disciple heard, felt comfort, or died; the passage does not establish those facts. This differs from CT-18, where a misunderstanding is tested and clarified.

**Evidence:** Single-scene case; E02 transfer is a boundary self-check, not independent support. Do not use a game passage to prescribe real-world care.

## Original transfer exercises

Use these prompts to test a technique in new prose. They are exercises, not evidence that the technique works. Several were self-reviewed only.

1. **CT-01:** Give two roommates the same small repair problem; one cares whether it works, the other how it is done. Let each judgment drive an action.
2. **CT-02:** After a strange encounter, let the protagonist check a clock and a held object. Each check should lead to an action without revealing the full mechanism.
3. **CT-03:** Let a child ask an adult to repeat a story line; later, let one character use it to make an ordinary decision without explaining its symbolism.
4. **CT-04:** In a decaying house, let one sensory detail change what a caretaker expects when a new person arrives.
5. **CT-05:** Give a witness a repeated gesture tied to a specific statement; let the player question or test it rather than accuse them from the gesture alone.
6. **CT-06:** After an item goes missing, let two characters offer explanations based on different observations and leave one fact unresolved.
7. **CT-07:** Let a character encounter an object used in an earlier attempt to delay a departure; have them ask what the other person wants now while preserving that person's reason to leave.
8. **CT-08:** On graduation day, pass an old jar among classmates; let two people handle it differently and end on an ordinary decision.
9. **CT-09:** As a storm closes the route to an old dock, offer questions about route safety, the missing classmate's reason for going, and whether a companion will go too. Answer each only with supported information.
10. **CT-10:** Two landing platforms are damaged and only one can be repaired first. Offer explicit priorities and let different staff respond to the cost they care about.
11. **CT-11:** Have roommates repair a stuck cabinet under a temporary rule; let an object change hands and function, then complete the ordinary task.
12. **CT-12:** Have roommates replace a kettle plug; let one complain about always repairing things and the other reveal a concrete worry about a nighttime outage. End on the work and a specific agreement.
13. **CT-13:** Let a character contact the player through a shared terminal and choose between two unverified explanations. The character should respond only to the answer actually received.
14. **CT-14:** A late message and an object corroborate each other. Let the protagonist ask what was hidden and inspect a record without having it decide whether forgiveness follows.
15. **CT-15:** During a departure argument, one person says “stop asking” and begins packing. The other may press, but show how that changes the next action; do not turn the refusal into secret consent.
16. **CT-16:** A graduate returns to an empty classroom, recalls one specific failure, and reconsiders a small current decision. End on an action, not a summary of youth.
17. **CT-17:** Have a graduate walk from the classroom door to their seat and then the lectern. Keep only a few details and let the final action answer a present need.
18. **CT-18:** As a horror boundary test, let a sound from a television that was just switched off repeat outside the door. Avoid listing theories or explaining the source.
19. **CT-19:** In a departure scene, let two earlier “we still need one thing” excuses lead into a direct request to stay; the other person can hear it and still leave.
20. **CT-20:** Write a magic-academy contest with a clear objective, prize, and limit. Let individual goals shape action and make the result concrete without forcing reconciliation.
21. **CT-21:** Write a bookshelf-repair scene where repeat inspection adds character interaction, not a hidden puzzle or extra choice.
22. **CT-22:** In a shared doorway scene, one character knows the “pear tree” code and another does not. Give the second a brief way to learn it locally, then use only shared information.
23. **CT-23:** On a next-day revisit, let the gardener remember whether the player allocated scarce water to a pear tree or vegetable seedlings. Borrow pruning shears only if both states allow it.
24. **CT-24:** Write one cooperative bookshelf repair with clear shared authorization, then a separate case where responsibility is unclear and a request is refused. Show what authority or accountability would change the refusal.
25. **CT-25:** Write a magic-academy duel with a few audience signals the viewpoint can observe. Do not let applause decide who won.
26. **CT-26:** During a crooked-bookshelf repair, let another person misread an unfinished workshop poster and prompt its creator to explain a small wish. Keep the humor in the shared task.
27. **CT-27:** Let roommates test an electric screwdriver on scrap wood, make a small recoverable mistake, and then finish the repair. Keep the cost real but limited.
28. **CT-28:** Let one roommate demonstrate using a level and shim; later, let the other apply the method independently under a slightly changed condition.
29. **CT-29:** Break bookshelf repair into alignment and backing-board steps. Let the beginner improve at one while another issue remains unresolved.
30. **CT-30:** During a singer's argument about a cancelled show, repeat one line until the partner counts it. Check that the count changes the conflict rather than importing a rehearsal gag unchanged.
31. **CT-31:** Let an old classroom habit pass from one class monitor to the next; use practical action and lightness to create a farewell, not a speech about legacy.
32. **CT-32:** Have a character directly ask someone to stay before they part. Let the answer acknowledge the request and land in a concrete action while preserving both people's choices; check whether direct speech fits better than mistaken identity.

## When a candidate may become a general rule

Only CT-01 and CT-02 began as cross-work candidates. CT-19 was added to the scene reference as an optional operation after one original transfer and an E04 branch-boundary check. CT-22 and CT-23 were added as optional interaction structures after cross-work local comparisons and boundary reviews. CT-24 became an optional scene check, not a main-entry rule; CT-25 became an optional presentation check. The remaining cards are case studies or bounded contrasts, not general rules.

Before proposing another general technique, write an original scene in a different genre and a counterexample where the operation should fail or should not be used. Check E01, E02, E03, E04, E06, E07, E08, E10, E13, E14, and E15 for whether the technique helps—and whether it instead turns daily comedy into paperwork, psychological horror into a fair puzzle, every voice into clever banter, or an exaggerated competition into an audience verdict.

These evaluations were author self-reviews, not independent A/B tests or reader validation. Later rounds reread existing transfer scenes and added CT-26 through CT-32 as case cards only; they did not add general rules. Do not describe self-review as independent evidence.
