# Ideation and Structure: Begin with the Desire to Keep Reading

## When to read this

Use when the user asks for a new work, a long-form outline, chapter rearrangement, or a rebuilt main plot. Do not start the full process for a few dialogue edits.

## 1. Find the story engine

Start with the real situation and ask: “Why would the player want to spend another scene with these people?” The answer may be curiosity, attachment, comic chemistry, fear, desire, exploration, or problem-solving. A theme may emerge later; do not require a thesis statement first.

Turn the premise into a relationship that keeps generating events. For example, one person wants to prove they no longer need help, while another wants to keep their place by being useful. Both may be kind, and neither may want to admit what they need. Relationship entanglement does not require everyone to be related, in a love triangle, or hiding a dark past.

Try three short scenes under different pressures: ordinary company, a blocked desire, and an opportunity. Can the characters produce different actions? If every scene needs an outside accident to move, repair the relationship engine before adding more worldbuilding.

## 2. Choose a structure; do not obey a structural label

| Structure | Experience it can support | Cost and common failure |
|---|---|---|
| Linear | Precise emotional accumulation, limited point of view, continuous presentation | Do not add cheap buttons just to have interaction; vary the reading experience |
| Common route plus character routes | The same situation developing differently across relationships | Do not reuse the same personality and conflict after merely changing the romance target |
| Local branches then convergence | Player participation in expression, local action, and emotional texture | Carry changed state into convergence instead of forgetting everything |
| Multiple viewpoints | Misunderstanding, parallel action, limited information | Do not use every switch to explain something the reader has just understood |
| Multi-route knowledge puzzle | New information changes how an earlier route is understood | Preserve the emotional value of earlier routes; avoid implying “everything before was fake” |
| In-world loop | Experiment, loss, and change under repeated conditions | Define what the protagonist remembers, which objects reset, and what each attempt costs |
| Cross-save unlock | Player experience becomes an entry condition | Define system memory, player knowledge, and character memory separately |

Structures can be combined, but state what each one contributes. When a new work is underspecified, start with the simplest skeleton that supports the intended experience; do not assume a replay-based true ending.

## 3. The smallest useful outline

For each chapter, record: entry situation, main desire, obstacle or shared activity, key action, exit change, visible plot progress, underlying change, daily-life or entertaining material, and important dependencies.

A chapter can pause the main plot while continuing a relationship or a sense of local life. A closed loop per chapter fits episodic cases or dungeons, but a long work does not have to split every chapter into “problem → solution → reveal of a bigger mystery.”

Write turning points as actions: “She leaves the spare key with a third person,” not “Her trust begins to waver.” The latter can be an intention, but cannot carry the plot on its own.

## 4. Callbacks and information release

Track the relationship among real event, character's version, what the player sees, possible misreading, and later verification. A key inference must be possible from the information currently available. A red herring needs its own cause; it should not exist only to fool the reader.

An important promise may involve a clue, relationship, image, or form. Note whether it needs explanation, fulfillment, transformation, or intentional suspension. Not every detail needs to return; distinguish deliberate suspension from an omission.

A second appearance of the same fact should add something: source credibility, consequence for action, a character's stance, or a changed meaning. Repeating “this matters” without adding anything usually calls for a cut.

## 5. Work backward from the ending

Try the key action first, then work backward to the ability, relationship, object, information, and opportunity it requires. Find where each was established. A climax may include a surprise, but a condition that solves its central problem should not appear from nowhere just when needed—unless the work has an explicit fable or absurdist contract.

Characters may fail, regress, or refuse to grow. An ending should answer the work's main promises; it need not explain all of the world's history or have narration grade the player's choices.

## 6. Scale and production

Estimate added burden from scenes, character designs, backgrounds, CG, voice acting, special states, and route-exclusive presentation. There is no fixed character quota; a temporary role may not need a full portrait.

Control branch cost with local convergence, shared events that carry different relationship states, and branching only at key scenes. Saving budget should not erase meaningful choices, and adding budget does not automatically add story value.

## Delivery

Provide an actionable outline and a few short samples, marking confirmed and tentative elements. If the user already asked for prose, keep this process as a brief internal work record; do not deliver an endless worldbuilding discussion in place of writing.
